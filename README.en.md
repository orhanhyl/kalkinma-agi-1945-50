## About this repository

This repository hosts the concrete, interactive outputs of the **relational,
material and multi-scalar analytical framework** — the core product of the
project above — and of the **actor-network mapping infrastructure** built on
it. The corpus consists of 1,465 documents with standardised catalogue
records, compiled from expert reports, diplomatic correspondence, archival
documents, newspapers and journals. Each map below was produced by coding the
development relations extracted from this corpus within the framework of
actor-network theory (ANT), associated above all with Bruno Latour, Michel
Callon and John Law, and of the material-semiotic approach.

## Research problematic

In the postwar period, Türkiye faced a choice between continuing the
state-led industrialisation policies that had been launched in the 1930s but
disrupted by the war, and integrating into the capitalist West, centred on the
United States, by embracing agricultural specialisation. In this period, the
proposition *“Türkiye has high potential but is an underdeveloped agrarian
society”* became the common reference point of development debates across
expert reports, diplomatic correspondence and press discussions.

The research does not ask whether this proposition is true or whose interests
it serves. Instead, it undertakes an **archaeology** of how the proposition
became an unquestioned fact with scientific standing — tracing how it
constructed reality, how it stabilised, and how it entered circulation as an
indisputable fact. In doing so, it places one question at the centre: **are
development practices really informed by policy, or do practices shape
policy?** Is the real work of development actors to design policy, or to turn
development into a stable and coherent reality that stands apart from events?
The project is organised around four objectives:

1. To develop an actor-oriented, transnational understanding of postwar
   development politics that takes local, national and international scales
   into account together;
2. To establish a mapping approach that treats development as an actor-network
   in relational and material terms;
3. To produce an actor-network map that visualises the relations among the
   human and non-human actors mobilised in the development network;
4. To set out the relationship between politics and expertise, together with
   its constituent elements.

## Findings

The findings show that the overarching proposition (P0) — “Türkiye has high
potential but is an underdeveloped agrarian society” — was not a ready-made
diagnosis to be found in the documents of the period, but a reality built step
by step through operations of measurement, classification, representation and
repetition. P0 is composed of three sub-diagnoses that complement one another
functionally — it is not the sum of independent observations:

- **P1 — High potential** is built through reserves, discharge figures,
  arable land and export projections. The Seyhan River engineering report,
  for instance, calculated flow regime, flood risk and dam cross-section
  together and thereby turned the river into 51,700 kilowatts of generating
  capacity; potential is always a category that is **calculated** under
  particular technical assumptions — not an accomplished success.
- **P2 — Underdevelopment** is built through a chain of deficiencies that
  refer to one another: inadequate roads raise transport costs, costs raise the
  need for foreign exchange, scarce foreign exchange constrains investment
  priorities, and the question of priorities in turn raises the problem of
  coordination. A concrete trace of this chain is the **twentyfold gap**
  between the 500-million-dollar credit Türkiye requested from the United
  States in 1945 and the 25 million dollars approved by the Export-Import Bank
  (Eximbank) in 1946 — underdevelopment here takes concrete form not as an
  abstract “lack of capital” but as a negotiated distance between the desire
  to invest and the capacity an external creditor deemed appropriate.
- **P3 — Agrarian society** is built through selected indicators (more than
  6.5 million of an economically active population of 8 million working in
  agriculture, subsistence consumption, low cash income, a shortage of
  equipment). Treating the traditional wooden plough (*karasaban*) and animal
  power as emblems of “backward” production does not reflect an objective
  attribute of the peasant; the decisive operation is the comparison of
  motorised-plough diagrams with implements driven by human or animal power,
  which places the latter in a position of deficiency.

These three diagnoses come to corroborate one another through repetition
across different document types (expert report, diplomatic correspondence,
archival record, press) — and this repetition gives P0 a stability that can be
explained through **Latour’s (1987) notion of black-boxing**: as a claim that
first appears as a technical diagnosis is turned into a subject of negotiation
in diplomatic correspondence, an administrative task in an archival record,
and public criticism in a newspaper column, it comes to be treated as settled
without its source being questioned. Yet this stability is not final: the
findings also show that the process did not produce a fully settled consensus.
Black-boxing is not a state in which objection has ceased, but a **selective
tendency** in which some claims are taken over without being reopened.

A direct consequence is that expertise is not a field of technical knowledge
independent of politics. The case of Max Weston Thornburg shows this clearly:
his authority was established not by technical texts written in a closed
office, but by his introduction through diplomatic channels and by journalists
relaying and headlining his views — that is, through **the moments of
problematisation, interessement, enrolment and mobilisation in Callon’s (1984)
sociology of translation**. Expertise thus appears as a **constitutive**
element that defines policy problems, sets priorities and legitimises the
instruments of implementation; politics and expertise are not two separate
fields but components of a single power-knowledge complex.

The making of development is also **multi-scalar**: the villagers’ demand for
a dam in the Seyhan basin, ministerial correspondence in Ankara, statistical
tables carried to Washington, and assessments by the International Bank for
Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) and the Economic Cooperation
Administration (ECA) are different links of the same chain of translation — a
single network stretching from the local to the transnational. Nor does this
network advance without friction: instances of resistance, such as the failure
of the Mercury–Medioli financing initiative to become a real construction site
once no reliable source of funds could be secured, undercut narratives that
present development as necessary, one-directional progress, and show that the
programme was kept alive through a series of negotiations that worked their
way around obstacles.

The interactive maps in this repository offer a way of exploring exactly this
finding: each map makes visible, and traceable back to its source, the
thousands of individual relations that build the P0/P1/P2/P3 propositions —
which actor, in which document, through which mechanism, enabled or impeded
what.

## Theoretical framework

This positioning does not claim to fill a gap. Türkiye’s early Cold War
development experience is already the subject of an extensive literature: the
security architecture, political-economic transformation, the on-the-ground
negotiations of technical assistance, expertise networks and cultural
representations have all been treated in detail. Development economics and
modernisation theory first defined the shared categories of the period —
potential, underdevelopment, the dual economy — and the dependency school and
post-development critique asked whose interests these categories served and
which power relations they rendered invisible. This research does not step
outside those debates to invent a new case; it traces how these separately
strong lines of explanation intersect in the making of one and the same
proposition — “Türkiye has high potential but is an underdeveloped agrarian
society”. The difference lies in the question: whereas earlier literature asks
whether this diagnosis is correct or whom it serves, this study traces how the
diagnosis became an unquestionable reality — which document corroborates which,
and which actor aligns which.

This has a concrete methodological consequence: the research is not built on
the classical plane of dependent and independent variables. The United States’
postwar foreign-policy goals, for example, are not treated as an independent
variable that directly caused Türkiye’s adoption of an agriculture-based
development orientation; they are regarded as part of a heterogeneous network
made up of knowledge, discourse, material instruments and social relations,
and development policies are explained through the interaction of the human and
non-human actors in that network. The unit of analysis is thus not causality
between fixed variables but the making and stabilisation of networks — for
which actor-network theory supplies the theoretical counterpart.

The research draws on actor-network theory. According to this theory, “the
social” — categories such as the state, institution or class — is not a
ready-made ground; it is a fragile assemblage that the relations among human
and non-human elements re-produce each time. Its methodological counterpart is
the **principle of generalised symmetry** (Callon, 1984), which commits the
analyst to describing human and non-human actors — documents, technologies,
institutions — in the same terms; in line with ANT’s relational understanding
of entities (Law, 2008), the properties and effects of an actor are not given
in advance but emerge only within the relations it forms with other actors.
The central concept of the theory, **translation**, is the process by which the
interests of heterogeneous actors are redefined and aligned around a common
network, and it proceeds in four moments: problematisation, interessement,
enrolment, and the mobilisation of allies (Callon, 1984). When such networks
reach a certain stability, **black-boxing** occurs — a complex set of relations
comes to appear as a single, unquestionable reality (Latour, 1987).

In this process the researcher adopts an **agnostic** position (Callon, 1984):
the claims of actors are not classified in advance as true or false; the
researcher traces the relations through which these claims gain strength.
Documents are treated not only for their discursive content but also for their
materiality (the material-semiotic approach; Law, 2008; Mol, 2002). The five
**macro-processes** of the coding system below (problematisation,
interessement, enrolment [assignment of roles and tasks],
inscription/mobilisation/stabilisation, and resistance and rupture)
operationalise these four theoretical moments of translation — together with
an added dimension of resistance — in document-based coding.

## Method: a relation-based actor-network coding scheme

This theoretical framework is translated into a concrete coding protocol
through the following steps:

### Process: from archive to map

**1. Archival ethnography.** The corpus is built by surveying and compiling
expert reports, diplomatic correspondence, archival documents, newspapers and
journals — **1,465 documents** with standardised catalogue records, a total of
**about 3.65 million words**:

| Type | Number of documents | Number of words |
|---|---|---|
| Archival documents (A) | 72 | 443,144 |
| Diplomatic correspondence (C) | 540 | 653,038 |
| Journals (D) | 533 | 679,400 |
| Newspapers (G) | 269 | 206,497 |
| Reports (R) | 51 | 1,670,196 |
| **Total** | **1,465** | **3,652,275** |

82% of the corpus falls exactly within the 1945–1950 window; the remainder
consists of antecedent (1943–44) and subsequent (1951–56) documents that
contextualise the period, plus one later-published memoir.

**2. Transcription.** The compiled archival material is converted into
digital text.

**3. Cataloguing.** Each document is systematically catalogued with its type,
date and source, and assigned a unique identifier.

**4. Candidate passage selection.** Within the catalogued documents, the
researcher screens for passages that are candidates for coding using a
**closed list of terms**, on the basis of the terms’ **co-word / co-occurrence
frequency**. *The function of this step is only pre-screening*: it narrows
down where to look in the large corpus, and makes no decision about which
relation exists or how it should be coded. A deterministic gate then applies a
final filter based on formal criteria.

**5. Relation-based coding (the main work).** From each selected passage, a
single relation triple is extracted using a **closed code/relation list**
built on the basis of actor-network theory:

> **X (influencer/source) — relation (mechanism of influence) — Y (influenced/target)**

This extraction — which two entities are related, in which direction, and
through which mechanism (enables, compels, impedes, finances, weakens,
constrains…) — is carried out on the evidence sentence in the passage through
**the researcher’s interpretation**. Deriving which closed code the relation
corresponds to, and looking up the source and target ends in a pre-established
dictionary of entities, are deterministic; when a new end appears that has no
counterpart in the dictionary, reducing it to an atomic core is again a
decision for the researcher. Finally, each coded relation passes through a
deterministic validation gate (schema, relation–code consistency,
traceability to the source text, atomicity).

The coded relations are classified under one of the five macro-processes
mentioned above.

**6. Proposition assignment.** Each validated relation is assigned to one of a
**closed list of propositions and sub-propositions** derived inductively from
the corpus — “Türkiye has high potential” (P1), “Türkiye is underdeveloped”
(P2), “Türkiye is an agrarian society” (P3) and their sub-divisions. The
assignment is made deterministically by rule matching; ambiguous cases that
overlap with more than one proposition are left to the researcher’s decision.
This layer forms the navigation axis of the maps in this repository.

### Originality of the method

Actor-network theory is better described as a family of sensibilities and
analytical tools than as a ready-made method (cf. Law, 2008), and it does not
by itself supply a procedure that can be applied directly to a large-scale
historical archive: it is one thing to say at the theoretical level that an
administrative decision functions as an obligatory passage point, and another
to specify by which criteria that function is to be identified across tens of
thousands of pages of documents. The project’s main methodological
contribution lies precisely here: translating ANT’s relational ontology into an
auditable coding protocol in which every relation can be traced back to its
source document and passage.

Many text-mining studies of development discourse rely on **co-word /
co-occurrence** methods (Callon et al., 1983): the frequency with which terms
appear together in a text is taken as *decisive* evidence that a meaningful
relation exists between them — fixed entities or categories are assumed first,
and their co-occurrence is measured afterwards. But two actors mentioned
together in the same report may be part of a conflict, a negotiation, a failed
attempt at translation or an ordinary administrative procedure; although
co-occurrence frequency points to the existence of a relation, it does not
explain its direction, nature or function. Other relational methods, such as
force-directed network visualisation and controversy mapping, meet a similar
limit: both depend on rich, continuous sources that leave digital traces (web
archives, online platforms), and neither is directly applicable to the
typewritten reports, handwritten notes and paper correspondence that make up
this research’s material. For this reason, the project uses
co-word/co-occurrence only as a **pre-screening tool in step 4, candidate
passage selection**; no relation is coded on the basis of co-occurrence
frequency alone.

Instead, proceeding from ANT’s relational understanding of entities —
**the properties of an entity emerge only within the relations it forms with
other actors** — a **relation-based coding scheme** has been built: a node
(actor or object) exists only when it is coded as one end of a relation; the
basic unit of coding is not frequency or co-occurrence but a **directed,
mechanism-bearing relation** (X — relation — Y) drawn from a closed code list.
This scheme was developed specifically for this project, translating ANT’s
relational ontology into a concrete coding protocol.

The same caution applies to how the maps in this repository are read. That a
node sits at the centre of a visualisation or lies close to other nodes is not
evidence that the actor was historically the most important one — it is a
representation produced by the layout algorithm from the number of coded
relations (Jacomy et al., 2014). The maps therefore do not offer a final
interpretation; they are an exploratory surface that helps the researcher
formulate new questions, notice unexpected patterns, and return to the
documents to verify them.

## Contributions and limitations

The study’s contributions fall at three levels: at the **theoretical** level,
a framework that re-reads development through ANT and the material-semiotic
approach; at the **methodological** level, an auditable research architecture
that makes every interpretation traceable back to its source document
(transcription → passage selection → relation-based coding → proposition
family → actor standardisation → network mapping); and at the **empirical**
level, a relational re-examination of a corpus of 1,465 documents. The research
acknowledges that it reaches the practices it represents not directly but only
through documents — a recommendation in an expert report is not an implemented
policy — and that coding and mapping are themselves operations that involve the
researcher’s interpretation. For this reason, the requirement that every
relation be supported by evidence and traceable back to its source has been
upheld throughout the study as a methodological safeguard.

## Transferability of the method

The relation-based coding and network-mapping scheme established here is not
tied to a single historical case. The same method can be applied to
development corpora from other periods and countries, opening them to
comparative readings; beyond development, it can be used for the relational
analysis of knowledge, expertise and decision-making in fields such as health,
the environment, education and infrastructure. One of the directions the
project proposed was to turn the actor-network infrastructure it built into an
accessible, interactive visual platform — this repository and the maps in it
are the concrete realisation of that proposal.

The method also carries an implication for policy analysis: the approach tested
here assesses the effect of a policy not through causality between fixed
variables but by tracing the relations among actors, institutions, documents
and objects. This relational view offers a basis for reading not only
1945–1950 Türkiye but also contemporary development and policy processes in a
context-sensitive way.

## Dissemination and impact

The research findings have been tested in international academic forums: the
paper “A ‘Good Neighbor’ for the Middle East: Constructing Türkiye as a
Development Mediator in the Early Cold War” was accepted as a full paper at
the ICRP: Middle East conference and published in its proceedings; “ECA and
European Recovery in Türkiye (1945–1950): The Limits of Alternative Development
Finance” was presented at the Tensions of Europe conference held in the
Netherlands; the paper “Black-Boxing Development: Documentary Infrastructure,
Relational Coding, and the Turkish Laboratory of the Early Cold War” was
accepted for the Society for Social Studies of Science (4S) conference in
Toronto, where it will be presented in October. A panel proposal based on the
findings has been submitted to the 5th National Political Science Congress of
the Turkish Political Science Association (Çukurova University, 19–20
September 2026); an article on the Mercury–Medioli financing initiative is also
being prepared for publication in a peer-reviewed, indexed journal.
